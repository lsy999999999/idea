  "text": "Upon excitation, a chiral\nnonracemic system may emit light with\ndifferent intensities for the left ( I \n L ) and right ( I \n R ) circularly polarized\ncomponents. This phenomenon, called circularly\npolarized luminescence (CPL) or less commonly circularly polarized\nemission, can be thought of as the emission counterpart of electronic\ncircular dichroism (ECD). More specifically,\nit can also be referred to as circularly polarized fluorescence or\nphosphorescence, depending on the related nonpolarized phenomenon. CPL has evolved in the last 10 years from being\nconsidered to be a rather specialized topic to be a technique now\nroutinely employed by hundreds of research groups worldwide. Indeed,\nin the past decade, the number of manuscripts reporting CPL studies\nhas increased by approximately 5 times. This growth was led by the\npromise of applications of CPL-active systems in several technologies\non one hand \n−\n\n\n\n  and by the commercial availability of a few options of instrumentation\ncapable of reliable measurements on the other hand \n−\n\n  as well as\nby the possibility of building relatively cheap equipment in the laboratory. \n−\n\n\n\n\n\n \n\nCPL is commonly observed from (small) organic molecules in\nsolution, \n−\n\n\n  in particular helicenes and helicenoids, \n−\n\n\n  1,1′-binaphthyls and other\nbiaryls, \n−\n\n  BODIPY derivatives, pyrene\nintramolecular dimers or oligomers, and\ncyclophanes. Small organic molecules\noffer the advantages of low cost, ease of functionalization and facile\nmodulation, and control of optical and chiroptical properties through\norganic synthesis. Beyond purely organic molecules, chiral metal coordination\ncompounds such as Ru II , Ir III , Pt II , and Cr III  complexes are often investigated. \n−\n\n\n\n  Among coordination compounds, trivalent lanthanide (Ln III ) complexes have found a special place since the dawn of CPL, given the very high CPL performances they can\nachieve, thanks to the magnetic allowed character of certain  f – f  transitions. \n,−\n\n\n\n\n  Another relevant case is given by aggregated organic molecules.\nVery high CPL activity is obtained by small organic molecules or polymers\nforming aggregates in solutions (due to concentration effects or to\nthe presence of a “poor” solvent) or in thin films. \n−\n\n\n\n\n\n  This family includes multicomponent systems where a stereochemically\nordered aggregation of achiral compounds is driven by the presence\nof chiral dopants. \n,\n  In these cases, it is fundamental\nthat the monomers increase, or at least retain, their luminescence\nupon aggregation (aggregation-induced emission, AIE, or aggregation-enhanced\nemission), instead of undergoing aggregation-caused\nquenching (ACQ), which prevents any CPL activity. CPL is nowadays\nalso commonly reported for other systems, such as nanoparticles, \n−\n\n\n  quantum and carbon dots (both functionalized with chiral molecules\nand intrinsically chiral), \n−\n\n\n  perovskites, \n−\n\n  and in general (nano)-structured\nmaterials including up-converting systems. \n−\n\n\n\n  Moreover, CPL can be generated by extrinsic mechanisms.\nAn example is the case of CPL stemming from a coupling between nonparallel/nonorthogonal\nlinear birefringence and linear fluorescence anisotropy in self-assembled\ndomains. \n,\n  This rather rare phenomenon leads to a so-called\nnonreciprocal emission, which is circular polarization of the emitted\nlight that is opposite for the two faces of the film. Another example\nof extrinsic mechanism-generating CPL is the Bragg circular phenomenon,\ndisplayed by luminescent chiral nematic liquid crystals (CNLC) or\nCNLC doped with luminescent compounds. \n−\n\n  In this case, their\nperiodic helical structure allows for a polarization-selective reflection\npotentially reaching extremely high dissymmetry factors.\nAs\nalready mentioned, the interest in CPL-active systems has been\nstirred by the promise of new possibilities in chiral optoelectronics\nand photonics as well as in bioimaging and microscopy and in security inks. \n,\n  In fact, in\nthe last few decades several new techniques and technologies based\non CPL have emerged. A particularly promising application of chiral\nemitters is in organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), where CPL emitters\ncan be used in the active layer (CP-OLEDs). \n,−\n\n  In such devices, a net circular polarization of the\nelectroluminescence is observed. CP-OLEDs are expected to increase\nthe efficiency of displays, where circular polarizers are used as\nantiglare elements, which currently cut out more than 50% of the emission\nfrom nonpolarized LEDs. Besides electroluminescence,\nemission can be excited by an electrochemical reaction in a process\ntermed electrochemiluminescence (ECL). CPL-active systems can in principle\nemit CP-ECL under these conditions. The most exploited ECL-active\ncompound is [Ru­(bpy) 3 ] 2+  (bpy: 2,2′-bipyridine),\nwhich emits red light upon electrochemical oxidation in the presence\nof an amine. In 1987, Gillard, Dekkers,\nand co-workers reported CP-ECL from an enantiopure Ru-based complex, \n,\n  and more recently, CP-ECL activity was also reported from a pyrene-based\norganic compound, exploiting the strong polarization of the excimer\nemission. \n,\n  Finally, since the early days of the technique,\nseveral examples of CPL probes for bioanalytes of relevant interest\nhave been reported. \n−\n\n  CPL is very sensitive to the conformational change\nof the luminescent probe; therefore, the CPL signal modification upon\nthe probe–analyte interaction is usually more pronounced than\nin the case of total luminescence. In the last few years, two new\ntechniques capable of substantially improving the possibilities in\nthe field were reported: CPL laser scanning confocal microscopy (CPL-LSCM)\nand two-photon CPL. CPL-LSCM allows one\nto track the CPL of a fluorophore with micrometer resolution (e.g.,\nwithin a cell or a complex material) and to differentiate between\nareas with different CPL signals or to exploit the so-called enantioselective\ndifferential chiral contrast in the case of enantiomer segregation. \n,\n  Two-photon CPL joins the advantages of CPL with those of two photon\nemission (i.e., lower-energy excitation and the possibility for higher\nresolution due to the emission quadratic dependence on the excitation\nintensity). Another breakthrough will\nlikely happen in the near future by merging the two-photon CPL with\nCPL-LSCM, allowing for an unprecedented high-resolution tracking of\nenantio- or diastereo-selective processes.
