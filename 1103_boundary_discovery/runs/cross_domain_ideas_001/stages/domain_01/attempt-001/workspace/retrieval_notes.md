# Evolutionary ecology source notes

Accessed 2026-11-04. Abstract text was retrieved from Crossref's public works API; the corresponding JSON responses are retained next to this note. Notes below distinguish source claims from the disciplinary interpretations used to construct the card.

## Post & Palkovacs (2009)

Source: “Eco-evolutionary feedbacks in community and ecosystem ecology: interactions between the ecological theatre and the evolutionary play,” *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 364:1629–1640. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0012. Retrieved record: `post_palkovacs_abstract.json`.

The abstract defines an eco-evolutionary feedback as a reciprocal loop: populations change their environment through niche construction, then that altered environment influences subsequent evolution. It names predation, nutrient excretion, and habitat modification as routes by which organisms change environments, and describes contemporary evolution on ecological timescales. Examples span rotifer–algae chemostats, alewife–zooplankton lakes, guppy life history and stream nutrient cycling, seed predation, and tree leaf chemistry/soil processes. The abstract calls the alewife–zooplankton case the most complete evidence among its examples.

## Hairston et al. (2005)

Source: “Rapid evolution and the convergence of ecological and evolutionary time,” *Ecology Letters* 8:1114–1127. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1461-0248.2005.00812.x. Retrieved record: `hairston_abstract.json`.

The abstract argues that ecological relevance, rather than a rate benchmark alone, should define rapid evolution: genetic change is rapid when it measurably affects simultaneous ecological change. It proposes decomposing ecological change into components driven by evolutionary change and non-evolutionary factors such as density dependence or abiotic change. In its worked examples, the estimated evolutionary contribution varies across systems: finch evolution is estimated as 2.2 times ecological change, while copepod evolution is about one quarter the ecological rate. Those values are source-specific illustrations, not general constants.

## Retrieval boundary

The abstracts and metadata were read; full texts were not reviewed. A preliminary lookup using a mismatched DOI returned an unrelated striped-mouse puberty paper and was excluded from the card. An attempted Europe PMC lookup for another review returned no match, so it is not cited. The two retained sources are sufficient for this disciplinary input but do not represent a comprehensive literature search.
